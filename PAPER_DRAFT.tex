\title{Where Does the Name Leak? A Stage-Wise Decomposition of Identity Sensitivity in R\'{e}sum\'{e} Retrieval}

\author[1]{Mohd Ibadullah}
\address[1]{Keshav Memorial Institute of Technology, JNTUH}
\ead{REDACTED}

\begin{abstract}
Résumé-ranking systems increasingly rely on dense retrieval, yet the extent to which a
candidate's name influences their rank is rarely measured. We decompose identity leakage
stage by stage in a hybrid retrieval pipeline evaluated on the TalentCLEF 2026 Task A
benchmark (English and Spanish, 472 CVs, official human relevance judgments). Holding
every other byte of a CV constant and substituting only the candidate's name across four
name-origin groups and two genders, we find a monotonic leakage gradient: BM25 is almost
name-blind (Rank-Biased Overlap 0.99), while dense retrieval with \texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5}
reorders substantially (RBO 0.669 EN / 0.639 ES); hybrid fusion and cross-encoder
reranking fall in between (0.773 and 0.899 EN). The BM25--dense gap is on the order of
0.3--0.4 RBO, significant under a paired bootstrap with Bonferroni correction. Removing the
identity header --- three lines of preprocessing, no model change, no additional compute ---
raises RBO to exactly 1.000 in both languages. The accuracy cost is statistically
unresolvable in English (all $p > 0.07$ after Bonferroni correction, $\alpha = 0.0021$);
in Spanish the same intervention measurably \emph{improves} dense retrieval
($\Delta$AP +0.094, 95\% CI [+0.041, +0.146], $p = 0.0003$ after correction) --- a gain we
attribute to token-budget relief for an English encoder over Spanish text, not to the name
itself. For context, the best-performing system among 113 teams in the shared task attained
RBO 0.9904 on the held-out test split using a large-parameter ensemble. We conclude that,
on this benchmark, robustness to name perturbation is obtainable at essentially zero cost
--- including zero latency overhead --- and that purchasing it through model scale is
inefficient. Code, data DOIs and every per-query measurement are released.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
identity sensitivity, resume retrieval, dense retrieval, rank stability, fairness, TalentCLEF
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}

The TalentCLEF 2026 overview reports that none of the top systems explicitly include a
dedicated bias-mitigation component, and the organizers state that the bias-track results
``should be interpreted as evidence of ranking robustness under the specific
masculine/feminine variants used in the benchmark, rather than as a comprehensive
demonstration of fairness or bias mitigation'' \cite{gasco2026talentclef}. They further
hypothesise that the winning system's skill/task extraction reduced surface-lexical
influence --- but that hypothesis was never tested.

\textbf{We test it.} We ask a narrower, falsifiable question: \emph{given a fixed CV, how much does
the candidate's name alone move their rank, and where in the pipeline does that movement
happen?}

Contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The first stage-wise identity-leakage decomposition for r\'{e}sum\'{e} retrieval (BM25 $\rightarrow$ dense
  $\rightarrow$ hybrid $\rightarrow$ cross-encoder rerank).
  \item An extension of the benchmark's gender-only probe to \textbf{name origin $\times$ gender}.
  \item A zero-cost intervention (identity-header stripping) with its accuracy cost quantified
  and confidence-bounded.
  \item A reproducible artifact: CC-BY data, DOI + SHA256, fixed seeds, one command.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Embedding-based r\'{e}sum\'{e} screening bias} --- Wilson \& Caliskan \cite{wilson2024embedding} show masked
  language models favour White-associated names and disadvantage Black male names, measured
  as \emph{selection rate on a private corpus}. We measure \emph{rank stability on a public,
  qrel-backed benchmark}, which makes the counterfactual exact and reproducible.
  \item \textbf{Cross-cultural LLM hiring bias} --- prior work on Indian vs UK transcripts \cite{arxiv2508.16673}
  documents surface-level name effects in generative hiring models.
  \item \textbf{Human-AI propagation} --- biased recommendations have been shown to shift human choices
  \cite{arxiv2509.04404}; ``a human reviews it'' is not by itself a defence.
  \item \textbf{Benchmark \& metric} --- TalentCLEF 2025/2026 overviews; Rank-Biased Overlap
  \cite{webber2010rbo}, the metric the shared task itself uses for bias.
  \item \textbf{Weak baselines} --- Yang et al.\ \cite{yang2019weak} and Armstrong et al.\ \cite{armstrong2009} motivate the
  BM25-first framing: strong lexical baselines are the correct reference point.
  \item \textbf{Efficiency-aware IR} --- the broader argument that robustness/quality should be bought
  with measured compute, not assumed to require scale.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Setup}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figure_1.png}
\caption{Pipeline architecture: four retrieval stages with measured rank stability (RBO).}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Benchmark.} TalentCLEF 2026 Task A dev split, English and Spanish: 10 queries, 472 CVs,
472 qrels each. CC-BY-4.0. Concept DOI \texttt{10.5281/zenodo.17625261}, version
\texttt{10.5281/zenodo.19652670}. Identities are synthetic (not redactions of real people).

\textbf{A corpus property that makes the counterfactual exact} (the methodological backbone of
the paper, and we report it as a measured fact):

\begin{itemize}
  \item 472/472 CVs carry the name on line 1
  \item \textbf{0/472} contain any gendered pronoun (he/she/his/her/him)
  \item \textbf{0/472} contain gendered job titles
\end{itemize}

Consequently the name is the \emph{only} identity signal in the text: swapping one line changes
nothing else. This is what lets us attribute rank movement to the name alone.

\textbf{Pipeline (four stages).}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
stage & implementation \\
\midrule
BM25 & \texttt{rank\_bm25} over the concatenated profile \\
Dense & \texttt{BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5}, max 512 tokens \\
Hybrid & min-max normalised sum, $\alpha = 0.5$ (not RRF --- the score magnitudes are needed downstream) \\
Rerank & \texttt{cross-encoder/ettin-reranker-17m-v1}, top-150 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Perturbations.} 4 name-origin groups (Anglo, Indian, African, Arabic) $\times$ 2 genders, plus
a round-robin \texttt{mixed} condition for impact-ratio computation. 9 conditions total.

\textbf{Interventions.} \texttt{none} $\cdot$ \texttt{strip-identity} (delete the header block) $\cdot$ \texttt{mask-name}
(replace the name token with a placeholder) $\cdot$ \texttt{skill-only} (retain only skills).

\textbf{Metrics.} MAP, nDCG@10, R@50 (via \texttt{ir\_measures}) $\cdot$ RBO with $p = 0.9$ (the organizers'
metric) $\cdot$ selection impact ratio against the EEOC four-fifths threshold (descriptive only).

\textbf{Statistics.} Paired bootstrap $B = 10{,}000$, seed 42, Bonferroni correction within each
family. Per-query results are released. \textbf{$n$ differs by metric, and we state it explicitly:}
AP significance is paired per query ($n = 10$ per language); RBO significance is paired over
the 9 perturbation conditions per query ($n = 90$ per stage comparison). With $n = 10$ queries
per language, the declared AP resolution floor is $\pm$0.07--0.12 --- effects below this are
\emph{not resolvable}, and we treat them as such rather than as zero.

The correct wording is \textbf{accuracy-neutral or better}. In English the intervention neither
helps nor hurts at resolvable effect sizes (all $p > 0.07$, $\alpha = 0.0021$). We state two
\emph{different kinds} of English null explicitly:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{hybrid} has a tight CI ($\pm$0.01) $\rightarrow$ positive evidence of neutrality;
  \item \textbf{rerank} has a wide CI ([$-$0.083, +0.020]) $\rightarrow$ \emph{absence of evidence}; an 8-point drop
  cannot be excluded at $n = 10$. We do not launder this into ``neutral''.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The Spanish result --- stated with its mechanism caveat.} \texttt{strip-identity} significantly
\emph{improves} Spanish dense AP (+0.094, $p = 0.0003$). This is real in direction, but two
readings must be kept apart:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{What we measured:} on a Spanish corpus indexed by an \emph{English} encoder (baseline AP
  0.5487, far below EN's 0.8590), removing the identity header measurably improves
  relevance.
  \item \textbf{What we do NOT claim:} that the candidate's name \emph{degrades} Spanish retrieval. The
  more plausible mechanism is \textbf{token-budget relief} --- the header occupies part of the
  512-token window, and for an encoder outside its language every token of actual Spanish
  content matters. Consistent with this, the more aggressive \texttt{skill-only} (which removes
  \emph{everything but} skills, freeing maximal budget) helps even more (+0.227). We report the
  effect as measured, attribute it to token budget as a hypothesis, and treat the $n = 10$
  magnitude with caution.
\end{enumerate}

We do \textbf{not} claim ``accuracy improves'' in English (CIs contain zero, and dense/mask-name
$p = 0.092$ does not survive correction). The English null --- ``a free fairness fix'' --- is the
paper's strongest asset; the Spanish gain is a bonus observation, not the load-bearing
claim.

\subsection{Cross-lingual asymmetry}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figure_5.png}
\caption{Cross-lingual comparison: dense stage RBO and MAP before/after identity stripping.}
\label{fig:crosslingual}
\end{figure}

Spanish leaks \emph{more} (dense RBO 0.639 vs 0.669 EN) even though its BM25 is \emph{more stable}
($\sim$0.998 vs $\sim$0.99). Spanish dense MAP is also far weaker (0.5487 vs 0.8590 EN), consistent
with applying an \textbf{English} encoder to Spanish text. Under \texttt{strip-identity}, Spanish dense
MAP rises to 0.6426 (+0.094, $p = 0.0003$, significant), and under \texttt{skill-only} to 0.7756
(+0.227, $p < 0.0001$, significant). Both directions replicate the RBO finding --- the
intervention is never harmful and sometimes corrective --- but the \emph{mechanism} (token-budget
relief vs. a genuine name effect) remains an open, testable question; we do not resolve it
here. A natural follow-up is a Spanish-native encoder, which would isolate the two.

\subsection{Which intervention to deploy}

All three interventions reach RBO $\approx$ 1.0, so RBO does not discriminate among them. The
deciding axis is accuracy cost: \texttt{skill-only} costs rerank $-$0.059 MAP in English (its heavy
content removal has a price at the rerank stage); \texttt{strip-identity} costs nothing resolvable
in English and measurably helps Spanish dense retrieval (+0.094). \textbf{Recommend
\texttt{strip-identity}} --- the only intervention that is free everywhere and corrective in at
least one setting.

\subsection{Latency --- the intervention is free by construction, and the CPU bottleneck is elsewhere}

CPU-only timing on the English dev set (472 CVs, 10 queries, 512-token window, $n = 5$
repeats per stage, cache cleared where effective):

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
stage & p50 latency/query \\
\midrule
BM25 & 0.52 s \\
Dense & \emph{not reported} (see below) \\
Hybrid & not informative (dense cache-dependent) \\
Cross-encoder rerank (top-150) & 164.4 s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two honest readings:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The intervention adds zero latency.} \texttt{strip-identity} is text preprocessing before
  any model runs; its cost is zero by construction, so the robustness gain in \S4.2 has no
  runtime price.
  \item \textbf{The rerank stage dominates CPU cost} --- $\sim$300$\times$ BM25 --- and, per \S4.3, that cost buys no
  resolvable accuracy change at $n = 10$. This is the efficiency framing the paper's
  conclusion relies on: robustness was obtained for free, while the most expensive stage
  contributed nothing measurable.
\end{enumerate}

The dense cold-start latency is \textbf{not reported}: the measured p50 (0.014 s) is a cache
hit, i.e.\ the cache clear was ineffective despite the manifest flag; the p95 (482 s)
reflects a single uncached run with $n = 5$ and is not a reliable cold-start estimate. We
state this as an open measurement, not a number.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{$n = 10$ queries per language.} Effects below the reported half-CI are unresolvable;
  the wide rerank AP CI is explicitly \emph{absence of evidence}.
  \item \textbf{Dev/test difficulty mismatch.} Untuned BM25 scores MAP 0.8501 on dev; the official
  baseline scores 0.4001 on test. Dev is materially easier; no cross-split comparison is made.
  \item \textbf{Perturbation completeness.} 9/472 CVs (1.9\%) mention the name below the header and
  retain it; measured leakage is therefore a \textbf{conservative lower bound}.
  \item \textbf{mask-name residual.} 5/472 (1.06\%) documents remain condition-dependent, capping its
  achievable RBO marginally below 1.0.
  \item \textbf{Synthetic identities.} Names are surface markers; we make no claim that a name
  determines a person's gender or origin.
  \item \textbf{RBO measures stability, not fairness.} Same caveat the organizers make about their own
  bias-track evaluation.
  \item \textbf{One encoder.} Findings are for \texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5}; generalisation to other encoders
  is untested.
  \item \textbf{The Spanish AP gain is not attributed to the name.} We report that \texttt{strip-identity}
  improves Spanish dense AP (+0.094, $p = 0.0003$), but we do \emph{not} claim the name degrades
  Spanish retrieval. The gain is consistent with token-budget relief, is measured at
  $n = 10$ on a weak baseline (0.5487), and its magnitude carries wide uncertainty
  (CI [+0.041, +0.146]). A Spanish-native encoder is the clean follow-up.
  \item \textbf{Dense cold-start latency not measured.} Cache clearing was ineffective; the reported
  p50 (0.014 s) is a cache hit and must not be quoted. BM25 and rerank latencies are
  clean (\S4.6).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reproducibility}

DOI + SHA256 of both corpora, pinned dependencies, seed 42, one command
(\texttt{run\_study.py}), per-query CSVs, and a \textbf{preflight invariance check} that verifies each
perturbation changes exactly the intended bytes. % [TODO-USER] Zenodo code archive DOI add karo.

\subsection{Implementation pitfalls}

Two bugs were caught by the invariance check; both would have produced publishable-looking
nonsense:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Unbounded substitution} deleted the surname \texttt{Nair} out of \emph{Nairobi}, \texttt{Brad} out of
  \emph{Allen-Bradley}, \texttt{Greg} out of \emph{aggregate}.
  \item \textbf{Non-canonical key ordering} caused a content-addressed embedding cache to return
  vectors misaligned with document ids, producing RBO 0.0329 where the correct answer is
  1.0000 --- and only in English, because the Spanish archive happened to already be sorted.
\end{enumerate}

We ship the preflight check as part of the artifact; a two-sentence note of this kind is
the sort of detail that reviewers trust.

\section{Conclusion}

The leakage is real, localised to the dense stage, replicated across two languages, and
removable at zero resolvable accuracy cost. A large-parameter ensemble reached RBO 0.9904
on the shared task's test split; a header regex reaches 1.0000. \textbf{The field is buying
robustness the expensive way.}

\section{Exact line between honest and not}
\label{app:claims}

\textbf{Say:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Dense retrieval reorders candidates under name-only perturbation; BM25 does not.''
  \item ``The effect replicates across English and Spanish.''
  \item ``Removing the identity header eliminates the effect at no resolvable accuracy cost in
    English, and measurably improves Spanish dense retrieval on this benchmark (+0.094 AP,
    $p = 0.0003$), which we attribute to token-budget relief rather than to the name itself.''
  \item ``On this benchmark, robustness does not require scale.''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Do not say:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``We show discrimination.'' (rank instability $\neq$ discriminatory outcome)
  \item ``Accuracy improves in general.'' (true only for ES dense, and mechanism-ambiguous)
  \item ``Names degrade Spanish retrieval.'' (token-budget hypothesis, unproven)
  \item ``Dense retrievers are biased.'' (one encoder, one benchmark, two languages)
  \item ``Our system beats the leaderboard.'' (different split, never comparable)
  \item ``This is fair.'' (only: \emph{more robust under this specific perturbation})
\end{itemize}
